Print-Ready Artwork in Dubai: What Files Do Printers Need?

Formats, resolution, CMYK, bleed, fonts and finish layers: the file requirements printers check before proofing, plus a checklist to run before you send artwork.

By the Flash Print Solution team16 September 20266 min read

In this article

  1. File formats printers prefer
  2. Resolution: enough pixels at the final size
  3. Colour set-up
  4. Bleed, trim and safe area
  5. Fonts, fine detail and finish layers
  6. Pre-send checklist
  7. If your files are not quite there
  8. FAQs

Printers ask for print-ready artwork because a file that looks perfect on screen can still print soft, trim badly or change colour. Print-ready simply means the file already contains everything production needs, at the right size, resolution and colour set-up, so it can go to proof without rework. This guide lists the requirements printers check, so you can supply print-ready artwork in Dubai with confidence or brief your designer precisely.

File formats printers prefer

A press-quality PDF is the standard handover format for almost every printed product. It locks fonts, images and layout into one file that opens the same way on any system. Other formats have their place, but each comes with limits.

FormatGood forNotes
PDF (PDF/X-1a or PDF/X-4)Almost all print jobsExport with bleed and embedded fonts; the preferred handover format
AI or EPSLogos, signage, cutting and engraving filesOutline text and embed or supply linked images
Packaged InDesign filesBrochures, catalogues, multi-page documentsInclude the links and fonts folders so nothing goes missing
TIFF or high-quality PSDPhotographic posters, canvas, wall muralsFlatten layers and check resolution at final size
JPEG or PNGSimple photo prints onlyCompression and RGB colour often cause problems; avoid for text-heavy work
Word, PowerPoint or online editor exportsDrafts and contentUsually need conversion; export as a print PDF with bleed where the tool allows

Resolution: enough pixels at the final size

For anything viewed at arm's length, such as business cards, flyers, brochures and packaging, images should be around 300 ppi at the size they will print. A photo that is 300 ppi at postcard size falls well below that once it is stretched across an A3 poster.

Large-format work is viewed from further away, so lower effective resolutions are normal. Banners and roll-ups are often produced from images of roughly 100 to 150 ppi at full size, and building-scale graphics lower still. The key is to check at final size, not at whatever size the file happens to open.

Logos, text and line art should be vector wherever possible. Vector artwork scales to any size without losing sharpness, which matters for signage, vehicle graphics and anything cut or engraved. Upscaling a small web logo does not add detail; it only enlarges the blur.

Colour set-up

CMYK, not RGB

Screens display colour in RGB, while most printing uses CMYK inks. Very bright RGB greens, blues and oranges cannot be reproduced in CMYK, so they shift when converted. Converting early lets you see and adjust the change before it surprises you on the proof.

Spot colours for brand consistency

If a brand colour must match closely across jobs, specify its Pantone reference. On offset, a spot colour can be printed with a specially mixed ink; on digital, the printer matches it as closely as the CMYK process allows. Name spot colours consistently throughout the file.

Blacks

Small text should be 100% black only (C0 M0 Y0 K100), so it prints crisp with no colour fringing. Large black backgrounds look richer with a rich black build, commonly around C60 M40 Y40 K100, but check your printer's preferred values, as too much total ink can cause drying and set-off problems.

Bleed, trim and safe area

Printed sheets are cut after printing, and cutting has a small tolerance. Bleed is the extra image that extends beyond the trim line, so no white edge appears if the cut moves slightly.

  • Bleed: 3 mm on each side is standard for small-format print. A 90 × 55 mm business card becomes 96 × 61 mm with bleed.
  • Safe area: keep text and logos at least 3 to 5 mm inside the trim line.
  • Booklets: allow a wider inner margin near the spine and, for thicker saddle-stitched documents, allow for creep.
  • Large format: requirements vary by product. Banners may need extra area for hems and eyelets, and roll-up banners have a section at the bottom that sits hidden inside the base.

Always ask for the template for large-format, packaging and vehicle work, as bleed and safe zones are specific to each product.

Fonts, fine detail and finish layers

Text and lines

  • Embed all fonts in the PDF, or convert text to outlines in logo and signage files.
  • Keep body text at 6 pt or larger; light and condensed typefaces need more.
  • Avoid hairlines thinner than about 0.25 pt, as they can break up or disappear.
  • Take care with small white text reversed out of dark or multi-colour backgrounds.
  • Check that Arabic text renders correctly after export, with letters joined and in the right order.
  • Test barcodes and QR codes at the final printed size.

Special finishes and cut lines

Finishes such as foil, spot UV, white ink and embossing are not printed from the colour artwork; each needs its own instructions. Supply them on separate layers or as named spot colours, drawn in vector at 100% of a single colour and aligned exactly with the design. The same applies to cut lines for die-cut stickers, shaped cards and packaging dielines. Label them clearly, such as "Cut", "Foil" or "Spot UV", and set them to overprint so they do not knock out the design beneath. More on setting up a gloss highlight is in our spot UV printing guide.

Pre-send checklist

  1. The document is at final size, with bleed on all sides.
  2. Important content sits inside the safe area.
  3. Images are high enough resolution at final size.
  4. Colour mode is CMYK, with spot colours named where needed.
  5. Fonts are embedded or outlined.
  6. Finish and cut layers are separate, labelled and in vector.
  7. The file is exported as a press-quality PDF, one file per item, pages in order.
  8. The file name includes the item, size and version number.

For the design side of the process, from document set-up to checking the proof, read how to prepare artwork for print.

If your files are not quite there

Many files arrive nearly ready, perhaps missing bleed or built in RGB. That is normal and fixable. Our print-ready file setup and artwork correction services handle resizing, bleed, colour conversion and finish layers, and every file is checked before proofing. Send your artwork with your enquiry and we will point out anything that needs attention.

Frequently asked questions

Can I send my artwork as a JPEG?

A high-resolution JPEG can work for a simple photographic print, but it is rarely ideal for designs with text, logos or precise colour. JPEG compression softens edges, and many JPEGs are saved in RGB. A press-quality PDF with embedded fonts and bleed is the safer choice. If a JPEG is all you have, send it and we will check whether it is suitable.

What is the difference between bleed and the safe area?

Bleed is the extra artwork that extends beyond the trim line, typically 3 mm on small-format print, so no white edge shows after cutting. The safe area is the zone inside the trim line where important content should stay, so text and logos are not cut too close. Backgrounds and images run into the bleed; text stays inside the safe area.

Do I need to convert my files to CMYK myself?

Converting to CMYK before you finalise the design lets you see any colour shifts, especially in bright blues, greens and oranges, and adjust them yourself. If you supply RGB files, the printer will convert them, but you may notice changes on the proof. For brand-critical colours, supply Pantone references so they can be matched as closely as the process allows.
