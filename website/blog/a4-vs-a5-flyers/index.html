A4 vs A5 Flyer Printing: Which Size Should You Choose?

Dimensions, content space, paper weights and distribution compared, so you can pick between A4, A5, A6 and DL for your next flyer campaign.

By the Flash Print Solution team16 September 20266 min read

In this article

  1. The dimensions, and how the sizes relate
  2. A4 vs A5 flyer printing compared
  3. When A4 is the better choice
  4. When A5 makes more sense
  5. Choosing paper and finish by size
  6. Design tips that work at either size
  7. FAQs

Flyer size affects far more than layout. It decides how much you can say, how people receive and carry the piece, and how far your budget stretches across the print run. Most buyers weigh up A4 vs A5 flyer printing, and the right choice depends on how the flyer will be handed out and read.

The dimensions, and how the sizes relate

The A-series sizes are designed so that each one is exactly half the one above it. That makes planning straightforward, because layouts scale cleanly and several smaller flyers can be printed from one larger sheet.

SizeDimensionsRelative areaTypical use
A4210 × 297 mmFull sheetProduct sheets, takeaway menus, notices, event programmes
A5148 × 210 mmHalf of A4Promotions, openings, handouts, counter stacks
A6105 × 148 mmQuarter of A4Vouchers, bag inserts, postcards
DL99 × 210 mmOne-third of A4Rack cards, service lists, envelope mailers

When A4 is the better choice

A4 earns its space when the reader needs information rather than a single message. Product specification sheets, takeaway menus, course timetables, property details and event programmes all benefit from the room to organise content with headings, tables and several images.

A4 also works when the flyer will be pinned up. On a staff room noticeboard, a community board in a residential building or a clinic waiting area, the larger format is visible from further away.

Folding A4 into a leaflet

For content that keeps growing, folding turns an A4 sheet into a simple leaflet. A half-fold creates a four-page A5 piece, while a tri-fold produces six DL-sized panels that fit rack displays and standard DL envelopes. It bridges the gap into brochure printing without the cost of a bound booklet. On heavier papers, ask for the sheet to be creased before folding so the fold stays clean and the ink along it does not crack.

When A5 makes more sense

A5 is the working size for most promotional campaigns. It suits a shop opening, a seasonal offer, a new service, a class timetable or a restaurant launch, where one clear message and a call to action do the job. Because it fits easily in a hand or a bag, people are more likely to take it and keep it.

A5 is also efficient to produce and distribute. Two A5 flyers come from the same paper area as one A4, and A5 stacks sit neatly on reception counters, café tills and exhibition stands. For door-to-door distribution in residential communities, where building management allows it, A5 slips easily under doors and into letterboxes.

Consider A6 and DL too

If your message is a voucher, an event reminder or a QR code to a booking page, A6 may be enough. DL, the tall slim format, suits service lists, rack displays in hotel lobbies and flyers mailed in standard envelopes.

Choosing paper and finish by size

Paper weight should match how the flyer will be used, and size plays into that choice.

  • 130 to 170 gsm gloss or silk: the usual range for handout flyers of either size. Lighter weights suit large distributions; 170 gsm feels more solid.
  • 250 to 350 gsm card: turns an A5 or A6 flyer into a postcard-style piece that people tend to keep. At A4, card is best kept for menus and notices that must stand up to handling.
  • Lamination: worth it for A4 takeaway menus and counter cards that are handled repeatedly.

Gloss makes photography vivid; silk and matte are easier to read under bright lighting. Our comparison of matte vs glossy printing goes into more detail.

Design tips that work at either size

  1. Lead with one headline. Readers decide within seconds whether to keep a flyer, so make the main message obvious from arm's length.
  2. Keep text in proportion. An A5 side holds a headline, an image, three to five short points and contact details comfortably. If you need more, move up to A4 or use both sides.
  3. Size QR codes sensibly. Keep them at least around 20 mm square with a clear border, and test them from the printed proof.
  4. Allow bleed and margins. Add 3 mm bleed on every edge and keep text at least 5 mm inside the trim.
  5. Use both sides. The reverse is ideal for a map, opening hours or a menu extract.
  6. Plan bilingual layouts early. Arabic and English versions of the same message need roughly double the space. On A5, one language per side usually reads best; on A4, the two can sit in parallel columns.

If you would like help building the layout, our graphic design services can prepare artwork for either size.

Choose A4 when the reader needs detail and A5 when you need one message to travel well; A6 and DL cover the gaps in between. Frequently asked questions

What size is an A5 flyer?

An A5 flyer measures 148 × 210 mm, exactly half the size of an A4 sheet (210 × 297 mm). When supplying artwork, add 3 mm bleed on every edge, making the document 154 × 216 mm, and keep important text and logos at least 5 mm inside the trim line so nothing is lost when the flyers are cut.

Is A5 or A4 better for handing out flyers?

A5 is usually the better size for hand-to-hand distribution. It is easy to hold, fits in a pocket or bag, and carries one clear message with contact details. A4 suits flyers that need more information, such as product sheets, menus or event programmes, or pieces that will be pinned to noticeboards or read at a desk.

What paper weight should I use for flyers?

Most handout flyers are printed on 130 to 170 gsm gloss or silk paper, which balances feel with ease of distribution. For a postcard-style flyer that people keep, 250 to 350 gsm card works well, especially at A5 or A6. Takeaway menus and counter cards that are handled repeatedly benefit from heavier card and lamination.
