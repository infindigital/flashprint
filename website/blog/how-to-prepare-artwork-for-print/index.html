How to Prepare Artwork for Print, Step by Step

A six-step workflow for print artwork: set-up, layout, proofreading, special finishes, PDF export and proof checks, with tips for Arabic and bilingual designs.

By the Flash Print Solution team11 September 20266 min read

In this article

  1. Step 1: Set up the document correctly from the start
  2. Step 2: Build the layout with print in mind
  3. Step 3: Proofread before anything else changes
  4. Step 4: Prepare special elements
  5. Step 5: Export a clean print PDF
  6. Step 6: Check the proof properly
  7. Common mistakes, and where to get help
  8. FAQs

Good print artwork is prepared, not just designed. The decisions that save time and money are made at the start, when the document is set up, and at the end, when the proof is checked. This step-by-step guide shows how to prepare artwork for printing without rework, with notes on the bilingual layouts and folded items that often trip up projects in Dubai.

Step 1: Set up the document correctly from the start

Ask your printer for the template before you begin, especially for packaging, stickers, roll-ups, vehicles and anything shaped. Templates show the trim, bleed, safe area, folds and cut lines, and designing inside them avoids rebuilding the layout later.

  • Create the document at the finished size and add bleed in the document settings, not as an afterthought.
  • For very large items, work at a consistent scale, such as 1:10, and tell the printer which scale you used.
  • Set the colour working space to CMYK if the item will be printed in CMYK.
  • Add guides for the safe area so every page respects it.

Step 2: Build the layout with print in mind

Place images as linked, high-resolution files rather than pasting screenshots. Use the vector version of your logo. Define brand colours once as swatches and apply them throughout, so a later colour change updates everywhere and the values stay consistent.

Think about how the item will be held and read. Text near a fold or spine can disappear into it. Thin borders close to the trim edge exaggerate every small cutting variation, so either move them well inside or remove them.

Folded and multi-page items

Folded leaflets are not simply divided into equal panels. On a tri-fold, the panel that folds inside is usually a few millimetres narrower so it tucks in without buckling. Saddle-stitched booklets need a page count in multiples of four, and pages are normally supplied as single pages in reading order unless your printer asks for spreads. If you are producing a brochure or company profile, ask for the folding or binding template before the design is finalised.

Step 3: Proofread before anything else changes

Content errors are the costliest mistakes because they tend to be found late and require reprints. Check them while the file is still easy to change.

  • Phone numbers with the correct country and area codes, email addresses and website URLs.
  • Names, job titles and spellings, particularly on business cards ordered for a whole team.
  • Trade name and address exactly as they appear on your licence.
  • QR codes, scanned with more than one phone and pointing to the right, live link.
  • Prices, dates and offer terms on promotional material.

Arabic and bilingual layouts

Arabic text pasted from a word processor into design software can break: letters may disconnect or appear in reverse order if the application or font does not handle right-to-left text properly. Use a font designed for Arabic, typeset it in software that supports it, and ask a fluent reader to check the final PDF, not just the design file. Plan bilingual layouts so English and Arabic sit in balance, with matching hierarchy on both sides.

Step 4: Prepare special elements

If the job has foil, spot UV, embossing, white ink or a custom cut shape, each needs its own layer or named spot colour, drawn in vector and aligned with the design. Large-format graphics may need to be split into panels with overlaps, and vehicle graphics must be built on an accurate template for the specific model. Detailed specifications for these, along with resolution and colour values, are covered in what files printers need.

Step 5: Export a clean print PDF

  • Use a press-quality or PDF/X preset.
  • Include bleed, and offset crop marks so they sit outside the bleed area.
  • Make sure images are not downsampled below print resolution.
  • Embed all fonts.
  • Export one PDF per item, with pages in the correct order.
  • Name files clearly with item, size and version, such as menu-A4-v3.pdf.

Open the exported PDF and inspect it at 100% and 200% zoom. Problems that were invisible in the design file, such as missing images, substituted fonts or broken Arabic, often show up here.

Step 6: Check the proof properly

A proof is your last chance to catch errors before production. Treat approval as a sign-off on the whole job, not just the look.

  1. Read every word again, ideally with a second person.
  2. Confirm size, orientation, quantity, material and finish against your order.
  3. Check that nothing important sits close to the trim or folds.
  4. Verify finish positions on foil, spot UV and cut-line proofs.
  5. Remember that a screen shows colour differently from paper. For colour-critical work, ask for a physical proof.

Approve in writing, with one person signing off on behalf of the team.

Common mistakes, and where to get help

These are the issues that most often send files back for correction:

  • Logos taken from a website or social media profile at low resolution.
  • Bright RGB colours that dull noticeably once printed.
  • No bleed, so backgrounds stop exactly at the trim line.
  • Text or logos placed too close to the trim.
  • Missing fonts replaced by a default typeface.
  • Designs made at the wrong proportions, such as an A4 layout sent for a roll-up banner.
  • Several versions of the same file sent over different channels.

You do not need to be a print specialist to supply good artwork, but a little preparation removes most delays. If you would rather hand over the technical work, our graphic design services can build artwork from your brief, and print-ready file setup can prepare existing designs for production. Share your files or brief and we will check them before proofing.

Frequently asked questions

Can I prepare print artwork in an online design tool or PowerPoint?

You can, but check the export options carefully. Many online design tools offer a print PDF export with bleed and crop marks, which is the setting to use. Presentation software usually works in RGB and does not add bleed, so those files often need adjusting. Send what you have and ask for a file check before proofing, so any fixes are identified early.

Who is responsible for errors after I approve the proof?

Once a proof is approved, the printer produces what was signed off, so errors in text, numbers or layout that appear on the approved proof are normally the customer's responsibility. That is why careful proof checking matters. Read every word, test contact details and QR codes, and confirm size, material and finish before giving written approval.

Should I send separate files for each language?

It depends on the item. A bilingual business card or menu is usually one file with both languages in the layout. If you are producing separate English and Arabic versions of a brochure, supply them as separate, clearly named PDFs. Either way, have a fluent reader check the Arabic in the exported PDF, as text can break during export.
