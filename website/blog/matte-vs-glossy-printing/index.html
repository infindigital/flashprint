Matte vs Glossy Printing: Which Finish Is Better?

How matte and gloss finishes change colour, glare, feel and durability, and which one suits business cards, brochures, menus, posters and packaging.

By the Flash Print Solution team16 September 20266 min read

In this article

  2. Matte vs gloss compared
  3. Which finish suits which job
  4. Silk and soft-touch: the middle ground
  5. Combining matte and gloss on one piece
  6. Practical tips before you decide
  7. FAQs

The finish on a printed piece changes how colours look, how the surface feels in the hand and how well it survives handling. Matte vs glossy printing is one of the first choices a buyer is asked to make, yet it is often settled by habit rather than by what the piece actually needs to do. This guide explains where each finish comes from, how they compare, and which suits the most common print jobs.

Where the finish actually comes from

"Matte" or "gloss" on a quote can refer to three different things, and it helps to know which one is meant.

  • The paper coating. Coated papers are manufactured with a gloss, silk or matte surface. Gloss paper is polished to reflect light; matte paper has a flatter coating that diffuses it; silk sits in between.
  • A varnish or coating applied on press. A thin clear layer, such as an aqueous or UV varnish, can add sheen or reduce it and gives light protection.
  • Lamination. A plastic film bonded to the printed sheet after printing. Gloss, matte and soft-touch laminates add real durability, stiffness and resistance to moisture.

A card on gloss paper and a card with gloss lamination can look similar, but the laminated card is far tougher. When comparing quotes, confirm whether "gloss" means the stock, a varnish or a laminate.

Which finish suits which job

Business cards

Matte and soft-touch laminates are the more common choice for corporate cards because they look refined and create a surface for spot UV or foil to contrast against. Gloss suits photographic cards, bright retail brands and designs where colour is the whole message.

Brochures, company profiles and catalogues

Text-heavy documents read more comfortably on matte or silk stock, especially under office lighting. A common approach for brochure printing is a matte or silk text section with a laminated cover, sometimes with spot UV on the cover title. Image-led product catalogues often use gloss inside to make photography look its richest.

Restaurant menus

Menus are handled constantly and wiped down between guests, so lamination is usually more important than the sheen itself. Matte lamination reduces glare under pendant lights and candles; gloss lamination makes food photography look vivid. Our restaurant menu printing page covers the stocks and laminates that stand up to service.

Posters and point-of-sale

Gloss posters look punchy up close, but in brightly lit malls or shopfronts facing strong sunlight, reflections can make them hard to read from certain angles. Matte or satin finishes reduce that problem. For poster printing, consider where the poster will hang and where the light comes from before choosing.

Packaging and labels

Gloss suits bright consumer products and gives a clean, polished shelf presence. Matte and soft-touch finishes are widely used for cosmetics, fragrance, chocolate and gift packaging, where a quieter, more tactile surface fits the brand.

Banners and exhibition graphics

Large format graphics face the same glare problem on a bigger scale. Under exhibition spotlights and camera flashes, a glossy backdrop or roll-up can bounce light straight back at the viewer, so matte or satin media and laminates are usually preferred for event graphics. Gloss still has a place on displays viewed up close, where colour intensity matters more than reflections.

Silk and soft-touch: the middle ground

If neither extreme feels right, two alternatives are worth knowing. Silk paper has a gentle sheen that keeps colours lively while limiting glare, which makes it a popular choice for brochures and flyers that mix text and images. Soft-touch lamination has a velvety, almost suede-like feel and a deep matte look; it is a favourite for premium business cards, presentation folders and packaging, although it can mark with oily fingerprints over time.

Combining matte and gloss on one piece

You do not always have to choose. Spot UV applies a clear gloss varnish to selected areas over a matte laminated surface, so a logo or pattern shines against a flat background. The contrast works because the two finishes are so different, and it is one of the most effective ways to add depth without extra colours. Our article on what spot UV printing is explains how to set up artwork for it.

Practical tips before you decide

  • Large dark areas: matte laminate on black or navy can scuff; ask about anti-scuff matte film for pieces that will be handled heavily.
  • Folding: heavy ink coverage or lamination across a fold should be creased first to avoid cracking.
  • Colour expectations: the same artwork will look a little brighter on gloss than on matte, so approve your proof on the finish you are ordering.
  • Writing and stamping: if staff need to write on or stamp a piece, such as appointment cards or vouchers, leave that side unlaminated on a matte or uncoated stock.
  • Consistency: if several items sit together, such as a folder and a brochure, keep the finishes consistent or choose contrast deliberately.

Neither finish is better in every situation; the right one depends on lighting, handling and whether the piece is led by images or by text. Frequently asked questions

Does matte or gloss make colours look better?

Gloss makes colours look more saturated and gives deeper blacks, which suits photography and bright designs. Matte gives a softer, calmer rendering of the same colours and removes glare. Neither is more accurate; they simply look different. Because the same artwork appears slightly brighter on gloss, it is worth approving your proof on the finish you intend to order.

Which finish is more durable, matte or gloss?

Durability depends mainly on whether the piece is laminated, not on the sheen. A laminated card or menu, whether matte or gloss, resists moisture, tearing and handling far better than an unlaminated one. Within laminates, gloss can show fine scratches while matte can scuff on dark areas; anti-scuff matte films are available on request for heavily handled items.

What is soft-touch lamination?

Soft-touch lamination is a matte film with a velvety, suede-like texture. It gives business cards, presentation folders and packaging a premium tactile feel and a deep, low-glare look. It works especially well as a base for spot UV or foil. It can pick up oily fingerprints over time, so it suits pieces that are kept and handled with care.
