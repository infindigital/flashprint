Foil Printing in Dubai: A Guide for Cards and Packaging

Hot, cold and digital foil explained, with the colours, stocks and artwork rules that make metallic finishes look crisp on cards and packaging.

By the Flash Print Solution team16 September 20267 min read

In this article

  1. How foil gets onto the page
  2. Comparing the three methods
  3. Foil colours and effects
  4. Choosing the stock for foil
  5. Foil on packaging
  6. Artwork rules that make foil work
  7. Combining foil with other finishes
  8. FAQs

Metallic foil catches light in a way no printing ink can match, which is why it appears on everything from executive business cards to fragrance boxes. Foil printing is also more technical than it looks: the method, the stock and the artwork all decide whether the result is crisp or disappointing. This guide to foil printing in Dubai covers the main foil techniques, the finishes available, and the design rules that make foil work on cards and packaging.

How foil gets onto the page

Foil is a very thin metallic or pigmented layer carried on a plastic film. Different processes transfer it to paper or board, and each one produces a slightly different look.

Hot foil stamping

A metal die, typically magnesium, copper or brass, is made from your artwork. The heated die presses the foil film against the sheet, and the foil releases wherever the die makes contact. Hot foil gives a bright, sharp result with a slight impression into the stock, and it works on a wide range of papers, including uncoated and textured boards. Because a die is needed for each design, it carries a one-off tooling step.

Cold foil

Cold foil is applied on the printing press. An adhesive is printed in the shape of the design, foil is laminated onto it and the excess is stripped away. It can then be overprinted with CMYK inks, which creates coloured metallics from a single silver foil. Cold foil is common on labels and longer packaging runs; it looks flatter than hot foil and does not press into the stock.

Digital foil

Digital foil, sometimes called foil sleeking or toner foiling, uses a printed toner layer as the adhesive. Foil bonds to the toner under heat, so no die is needed. It suits short runs and personalised pieces, such as individual names on invitations or business cards. The result is flat and bright, though very large solid areas and extremely fine detail are harder to achieve than with hot foil.

Foil colours and effects

Gold and silver are the familiar choices, but the range is much wider. Popular options include:

  • Metallic foils: bright gold, matte gold, silver, rose gold, copper and bronze.
  • Holographic foils: rainbow or patterned effects that shift as the piece moves.
  • Coloured metallics: blue, red, green and other shades with a metallic sheen.
  • Pigment foils: solid, non-metallic colours such as white or black, useful for printing a light colour onto dark or kraft stock.

Brand colour guidelines rarely list a foil, so it helps to pair the foil with the palette rather than trying to match an ink colour. Warm golds suit navy, black, deep green and burgundy; silver and holographic suit cool greys and blues; copper and rose gold work well on cream, blush and kraft. Exact foil shades are confirmed with your quote.

Choosing the stock for foil

The surface under the foil has a big effect on sharpness. Smooth coated board and matte or soft-touch lamination give the crispest edges, which is why matte laminated cards with a gold logo are such a common combination; our gold foil business cards use exactly this approach. Soft-touch lamination makes the foil stand out even more against a velvety background.

Uncoated, cotton and textured stocks can take hot foil beautifully, with a slightly handmade character, but fine lines may break over the texture. Kraft board looks striking with gold, copper or white pigment foil. Dark stocks are ideal for foil, since metallic colours read most strongly against black, navy and deep colours.

Foil on packaging

On packaging, foil is usually used as an accent rather than a flood: the brand name, a crest, a border or a product name. It is widely used on cosmetic cartons, fragrance and chocolate boxes, gift bags and labels. For custom box printing, foil is typically applied to the flat printed sheet before the box is die-cut and assembled, so the foil position must be planned against the dieline.

Think about handling as well. Boxes travel, stack and rub against each other, and lamination under the foil improves resistance to scuffing. For broader packaging choices, including stock, structure and finishes together, see our product packaging printing page.

Artwork rules that make foil work

  1. Put foil on its own layer, set as a 100% spot colour in vector form and clearly named, for example "FOIL GOLD". Supply one layer per foil colour.
  2. Keep it solid. Foil is either on or off, so gradients, tints and photographs cannot be reproduced in foil.
  3. Avoid hairlines and tiny type. Very fine lines and small serif text can fill in or break; keep strokes and counters generous, particularly on textured stock.
  4. Allow registration tolerance. Foil may sit fractionally off the printed colours, so avoid designs where foil must fit inside a thin printed outline.
  5. Be cautious with large solids. Big flat areas of hot foil can show slight unevenness; a pattern or outline often looks better than a solid block.
  6. Keep foil clear of folds and trims. Foil can crack across a crease, so leave a margin around score lines and the cut edge.

Combining foil with other finishes

Foil pairs well with other embellishments. Foil with blind embossing raises the metallic area for extra depth. Foil with debossing presses it into a thick stock. Foil alongside spot UV lets you mix metallic highlights with clear gloss patterns; our guide on what spot UV printing is explains how that finish is set up. Each extra finish adds a separate production step, so it is worth choosing combinations deliberately rather than adding every option.

Foil works best when it is used with intent: a single metallic logo on a well-chosen stock usually says more than a design covered in shine. Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between hot foil and digital foil?

Hot foil uses a heated metal die, made from your artwork, to press foil onto the stock. It gives a crisp result with a slight impression and works on many papers, including textured ones. Digital foil bonds foil to a printed toner layer, so no die is needed, which suits short runs and personalised names. Digital foil looks flat and has more limits on fine detail.

Can foil be printed on kraft or dark paper?

Yes. Foil is opaque, so it shows strongly on dark and kraft stocks where standard CMYK inks would disappear. Gold, copper and silver look striking on black, navy and kraft, and white pigment foil is a useful way to add light-coloured text or logos to a dark or brown background. We confirm stock and foil compatibility with your quote.

How should I prepare artwork for foil printing?

Place all foil elements on a separate layer as solid 100% spot colour vector shapes, one layer per foil colour, clearly named. Avoid gradients, tints, photographs and very fine lines, and keep foil clear of folds and trim edges. Supply the file as a PDF with 3 mm bleed. We check the foil layer before proofing and flag anything that needs adjusting.
